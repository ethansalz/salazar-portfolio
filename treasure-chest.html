Treasure Chest LED Cooler

A $6 Goodwill treasure chest restored and converted into a functioning light-up drink cooler with motion-activated LEDs and decorative loot. Built almost entirely from garage materials for under $30 total.

Role Design, restoration, fabrication, wiring, finishing
Timeline Weekend build · Personal project
Key Materials Plywood, plastic tote, XPS foam, spray paint, Mod Podge, motion-activated LED strip, fake coins & gems, L-brackets

Build Summary

  • Goodwill treasure chest ($6)
  • Custom-cut plywood floor replacement
  • Trimmed plastic tote as removable basin
  • XPS foam gap fillers with painted finish
  • Motion-activated LED strip (purchased)
  • Fake coins & gems for detailing (purchased)
  • L-brackets for structural support

Cost Breakdown

  • Treasure chest: $6
  • LED strip bundle: ~$12
  • Fake loot: ~$8
  • Plywood, tote, foam, paint, hardware: $0 (on hand)
  • Total: under $30

Design Priorities

  • Novelty and visual effect over thermal performance
  • Removable tote for easy ice loading and cleanup
  • Minimal purchased materials
  • No permanent modifications to a real cooler

Overview

I found a treasure chest at Goodwill for $6. It was old, dirty, and had a hole in the bottom where the floor had separated at a corner. Most people would have passed on it, but I saw a cooler.

Using largely materials I already had in the garage, I restored the chest and turned it into a functioning light-up drink cooler with treasure-themed detailing. The only things I had to buy were fake coins and gems and a motion-controlled LED strip. Total cost came in under $30.

Floor Replacement

The original floor was damaged beyond repair, so I ripped it out entirely and custom-cut a sheet of plywood to fit the interior footprint. I secured the new floor using wood glue and nails, giving the chest a solid, flat base to build on.

Cooler Basin

I took a spare plastic tote from the garage that fit inside the chest and cut its height down using an electric saw so it would sit below the rim. I spray painted it black with multiple layers plus a food-safe clear coat so it could hold ice and drinks without looking like a storage bin.

The tote is fully removable, which makes it easy to load ice, add drinks, and pull the whole thing out for cleanup. No permanent cooler had to be cut up.

Foam Gap Fillers & Detailing

The tote didn't fill the full width of the chest, leaving gaps on the left and right sides. I cut leftover XPS foam to 3 inches wide and the length of the chest, stacking 3 inches of thickness on each side. I coated the foam with Mod Podge, painted it black, and glued fake coins and gems onto the top surface to create the treasure loot look.

I installed L-brackets to make sure the foam wouldn't shift or fall. Each foam piece is supported by the bracket below, the tote on one side, and the chest wall on the other. Stable and clean.

Motion-Activated LED Lighting

I installed a motion-detected LED strip bundle around the interior perimeter of the chest. When someone opens the lid, the LEDs light up automatically, giving it a movie-style treasure chest glow effect. The sensor and LED strips are mounted with adhesive backing, so no drilling or permanent modification was needed.

What I Learned

  • Design within constraints: the whole project was built around what I already had. The tote, foam, plywood, paint, and hardware were all garage inventory. That forced creative problem-solving at every step.
  • Finishing matters: multiple coats of spray paint, Mod Podge sealing, and the clear coat on the tote are what make it look intentional instead of thrown together.
  • Removability is a feature: designing the tote to lift out made the whole system practical. Without that, loading ice and cleaning up would have been miserable.
  • Novelty has value: this was designed for effect and fun, not thermal efficiency. Sometimes the point of a build is just to make something cool.
